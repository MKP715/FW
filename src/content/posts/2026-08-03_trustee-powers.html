Trustee Powers. What does it mean?

When you create a trust, one of the most important decisions you’ll make is what powers to give your trustee. A trustee can only act within the authority you grant — no more, no less. That means the powers you include in your trust document will determine how effectively your trustee can manage assets, protect property, and carry out your wishes.

A trustee must follow the terms of the trust, and they are also bound by fiduciary duties imposed by law. A fiduciary is someone who must put the beneficiary’s interests ahead of their own. These duties are recognized across legal and regulatory frameworks, including federal guidance on fiduciary responsibilities: IRS – Retirement Plan Fiduciary Responsibilities

Some clients want to give their trustee broad discretion. Others prefer to limit or narrowly define the trustee’s authority. There is no one‑size‑fits‑all answer. The “right” powers depend on your goals, your beneficiaries, and the complexity of your estate.

Below is a clear, plain‑English overview of common trustee powers and what they mean in real life.

Trustee Powers – A Comprehensive List.

This post provides a list of trustee powers one might consider. Keep in mind that not all estates nor grantors are created equal, therefore not all powers on this list will be right for you. It’s important to think carefully about your needs before including them in your trust. Make sure your estate planning attorney understands your goals. For additional trustee powers, see the California Probate Code.

Administrative Powers

A trustee must be able to handle the daily affairs of the trust. Some tasks include executing documents, signing tax returns, legal filings, and financial documents, hiring professionals e.g., attorneys, CPAs, investment advisors, appraisers, realtors.

Banking Powers

Trustees often need flexibility to manage cash flow and financial accounts, such as opening, closing, and managing financial accounts.

Investment Powers

Trustees must manage trust assets prudently and in alignment with the trust’s goals, such as buying, selling, exchanging, or retaining securities and hiring appropriate investment managers.

Real Property Powers

Real estate requires active management and decision‑making, such as managing leasehold properties, and deciding if and when to sell the primary residence, securing property managers, paying mortgages, taxes, insurance, and maintenance.

Business & Entity Powers

If the trust owns a business, the trustee may need authority to operate, manage, or liquidate the business, vote stock and exercise shareholder rights, and hire or fire employees.

Contract & Transactional Powers

Trustees often need broad authority to transact on behalf of the trust, such as entering into contracts, leases, and service agreements.

Insurance & Risk Management Powers

To protect trust assets, trustees may need to purchase and maintain liability, property, casualty, or umbrella insurance, maintain life insurance policies owned by the trust, file and manage insurance claims.

Tax Powers

Tax compliance is a core fiduciary responsibility of trustees. That would include preparing and filing federal, state, and local tax returns, making appropriate tax elections, paying taxes, penalties, and interest, hiring tax professionals.

Digital Asset Powers

This is one of the most important powers a trustee can have. An effective trustee needs the ability to access, manage, or close digital accounts (e.g., email, social media, cloud storage), access digital financial accounts (e.g., online banking, crypto wallets, PayPal, Venmo), manage websites, domain names, online stores, and more. It’s virtually impossible for a trustee to manage assets if she cannot access them.

Personal Property Powers

Trustees may need to manage, appraise, sell, distribute, or store personal property, such as jewelry, high value artwork, firearms, vintage items, and more .

Discretionary Distribution Powers

These powers determine how and when beneficiaries receive support, such as special needs beneficiaries, special gifts, making in‑kind distributions.

Beneficiary Protection Powers

These powers help trustees protect vulnerable beneficiaries, such as creating sub-trusts for minors, special needs, or spendthrift beneficiaries, delaying distributions based on milestones, paying expenses directly instead of giving cash outright to a beneficiary.

Environmental Powers

Especially relevant for California real estate where the issue might be to inspect property for environmental hazards, complying with environmental laws.

Charitable Giving Powers

If the trust includes charitable intent the trustee must be able to make charitable gifts, managing donor‑advised funds, and continuing the deceased’s gift giving choices.

Litigation & Settlement Powers

Sometimes a trustee may need to initiate or defend lawsuits, hire litigation attorneys, settle claims.

Powers of Appointment

If granted, the trustee may exercise limited or general powers of appointment.

Termination & Wind‑Up Powers

When the trust ends, the trustee must wind up trust affairs, make all final payments, debts, and expenses, prepare final accountings, and distribute remaining assets.

Catch‑All Power

Often trusts will include a broad residual power such as the power to “Do all acts necessary or advisable for the proper management, investment, and distribution of the trust estate.” This protects the trustee in the event necessary specific powers were accidentally omitted.

Final Thoughts

Choosing trustee powers is not a one‑size‑fits‑all decision. The right powers give your trustee the tools they need to manage your estate effectively. A well‑drafted trust protects your assets, supports your beneficiaries, and ensures your wishes are carried out with clarity and confidence.
