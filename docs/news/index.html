The law is racingto catch up with AI.

Who owns what a machine makes? Can someone clone your voice? What happens to your likeness after you're gone? Here are the developments that matter to creators, entrepreneurs and families — each with a plain-English note on why it matters.

AI & MusicOct 6, 2026

North Carolina man sentenced to 18 months in first-of-its-kind AI music streaming fraud case

Michael Smith of Cornelius, N.C., was sentenced by U.S. District Judge John G. Koeltl to 18 months in prison and two years of supervised release, and ordered to forfeit more than $8 million. Smith had pleaded guilty to conspiracy to commit wire fraud for using thousands of automated bot accounts to stream AI-generated songs on major platforms from 2017 to 2024, collecting more than $8 million in royalties.

Why it matters: Streaming royalties are paid from a shared pool, so fake streams take money directly from real artists and songwriters. Smith was punished for the fraud — not for making music with AI.

AI & CopyrightSeptember 2026

Third Circuit affirms: training ROSS's AI on Westlaw headnotes was not fair use

In Thomson Reuters v. ROSS Intelligence, the Third Circuit affirmed a 2025 ruling that ROSS infringed by using Westlaw headnotes to train a competing, non-generative legal-research AI. The court found the headnotes copyrightable and ROSS's commercial use harmful to Westlaw's market — including the emerging market for licensing AI training data. Commentators call it the first federal appellate fair-use ruling on AI training, though the court set generative-AI cases aside.

Why it mattersIt's the first appeals-court signal that copying content to train a competing AI product can fail fair use — and that licensing your content as training data is a real market worth protecting.
The National Law ReviewRead source

AI & MusicSep 18, 2026

Sony Music and Universal sue Suno again, targeting its new v6 model

Sony Music and Universal Music Group filed a second copyright suit against AI music generator Suno, aimed at its newly launched v6 model. The complaint alleges v6 was trained partly on outputs of Suno's earlier, allegedly infringing models — what the labels call “model laundering.” Suno says v6 was trained from scratch without their recordings. The labels' original 2024 case remains pending.

Why it mattersThe case tests whether an AI company can escape an infringement claim by training a new model on an old model's output — a question that matters to anyone licensing or using AI music tools.
VarietyRead source

AI & CopyrightSeptember 2026

NYT v. OpenAI & Microsoft reaches summary judgment as DOJ backs AI fair-use defense

Summary-judgment briefing is under way in The New York Times' copyright suit against OpenAI and Microsoft, part of a consolidated proceeding before U.S. District Judge Sidney Stein in Manhattan. The Justice Department filed a non-binding statement of interest arguing that training large language models on copyrighted works is fair use; the newspaper plaintiffs asked the court to give it no weight.

Why it mattersThis is the highest-profile test of whether AI companies can train on journalism without a license — and the federal government taking a side is unusual.
AxiosRead source

AI & CopyrightJul 20, 2026

Court grants final approval to $1.5 billion authors' settlement in Bartz v. Anthropic

A federal court in the Northern District of California granted final approval to the $1.5 billion class settlement resolving claims that books were downloaded from pirate libraries to build an AI training library. The settlement pays roughly $3,000 per work for about 480,000 works and requires destruction of the pirated files. It followed a 2025 ruling that training on lawfully acquired books was fair use, but keeping a library of pirated copies was not.

Why it mattersHow an AI company acquires its training data — not just how it uses it — can create billion-dollar liability. It's also a powerful reminder of why registering your copyrights matters.
The Authors GuildRead source

Right of PublicityJun 18, 2026

Revised NO FAKES Act clears Senate Judiciary Committee

The Senate Judiciary Committee approved a revised, bipartisan NO FAKES Act by voice vote. The bill would create a federal right to control digital replicas of a person's voice and visual likeness, with a notice-and-takedown system, counter-notices, exemptions for news and satire, and post-mortem rights of up to 70 years. It still needs a full Senate vote and House action.

Why it mattersIf enacted, performers, creators and families would gain a nationwide way to stop unauthorized AI voice and likeness clones — and it would change how state laws like California's apply.
Office of U.S. Sen. Marsha BlackburnRead source

Privacy & TechMay 19, 2026

FTC begins enforcing the TAKE IT DOWN Act's 48-hour removal rule

The TAKE IT DOWN Act, signed in May 2025, made it a federal crime to publish nonconsensual intimate images — real or AI-generated — and gave platforms a year to build a removal process. As of May 19, 2026, covered platforms must remove such images and known copies within 48 hours of a valid request. The FTC launched a complaint portal and sent compliance letters to major platforms.

Why it mattersVictims of deepfake imagery now have a federal takedown right with a deadline, and any business that hosts user content needs a compliant removal process.
Federal Trade CommissionRead source

Val Kilmer returns via AI in a new film — with his estate's consent

The trailer for the historical drama “As Deep as the Grave” features an AI recreation of the late Val Kilmer, who had been cast years earlier but was too ill to film. The filmmakers said his estate approved the project, supplied materials and was compensated, and that the production followed SAG-AFTRA standards.

Why it mattersYour voice and likeness can outlive you. A modern estate plan should say who controls them, and on what terms.

AI & CopyrightMar 25, 2026

Supreme Court narrows contributory copyright liability in Cox v. Sony

The Court reversed a roughly $1 billion jury verdict against internet provider Cox Communications, holding that a provider is not contributorily liable merely for continuing to serve subscribers it knows are infringing. Liability requires culpable intent — such as inducing infringement or offering a service tailored to it.

Why it mattersIt's now harder to hold internet and tech platforms — including AI services — responsible for their users' infringement, which changes enforcement strategy for creators.
Supreme Court of the United StatesRead source

Privacy & TechFeb 17, 2026

Federal judge: a defendant's chats with a consumer AI chatbot aren't privileged

In United States v. Heppner (S.D.N.Y.), Judge Jed Rakoff issued a written opinion holding that a criminal defendant's conversations with a consumer AI chatbot were not protected by attorney-client privilege or the work-product doctrine.

Why it mattersTyping your legal situation into a public AI tool is not the same as talking to your lawyer. Those conversations may be discoverable — talk to counsel first.

Right of PublicityDec 11, 2025

New York enacts AI “synthetic performer” ad disclosure and post-mortem likeness laws

Governor Hochul signed legislation requiring advertisers to disclose when ads use AI-generated “synthetic performers,” with civil penalties for violations. She also signed a law requiring consent from heirs or executors before a deceased person's name, image or likeness is used commercially.

Why it mattersAdvertisers reaching New Yorkers must label AI “people,” and estates gain more control over a loved one's likeness after death.
Office of New York Governor Kathy HochulRead source

Trademark & USPTONov 28, 2025

USPTO rescinds 2024 AI inventorship guidance and issues a revised standard

The U.S. Patent and Trademark Office published revised inventorship guidance for AI-assisted inventions, rescinding its 2024 guidance. AI systems cannot be named as inventors and are treated as tools; the same inventorship standard applies whether or not AI was used.

Why it mattersApplicants who use AI must be able to show that a human conceived the invention — the same human-centered principle that runs through copyright law.
Federal RegisterRead source

AI & MusicNov 25, 2025

Warner Music Group settles with Suno and partners on licensed AI music

WMG settled its 2024 copyright suit against Suno and formed a partnership under which Suno will launch licensed models and add download limits. The deal includes opt-in controls for artists and songwriters over their names, images, likenesses, voices and compositions.

Why it mattersIt points toward a future where AI music is licensed and artists opt in — affecting how musicians are paid and whether they can control voice cloning.
Rolling StoneRead source

AI & CopyrightNov 4, 2025

UK High Court largely rejects Getty Images' claims against Stability AI

In Getty Images v. Stability AI, the High Court rejected Getty's secondary copyright claim, finding that Stable Diffusion's model weights do not store copies of Getty's works. Getty succeeded only on a narrow trademark point about watermarks in some older outputs. Getty had dropped its main training claims during trial, so whether AI training itself infringes was not decided.

Why it mattersA leading non-U.S. ruling that an AI model isn't itself an “infringing copy” — and a reminder that where training happens, and what evidence you have, can decide these cases.
Courts and Tribunals Judiciary (UK)Read source

AI & MusicOct 29, 2025

Universal Music settles with Udio and plans a licensed AI music platform

UMG and AI song generator Udio announced a settlement and new licenses for recorded music and publishing, with plans for a subscription creation-and-streaming platform. UMG said artists would be paid both for training and for fan creations using their work.

Why it mattersMajor labels are turning lawsuits into licensing deals — setting expectations for how AI companies must pay rights holders.

Estate PlanningOct 16, 2025

OpenAI pauses Sora videos of Martin Luther King Jr. at his estate's request

After users created disrespectful AI videos of Dr. King with the Sora app, OpenAI paused generations of his likeness in a joint statement with the King estate, and said representatives of other historical figures can ask for their likenesses to be excluded.

Why it mattersFamilies and estates increasingly have to police a loved one's likeness online. Naming who controls those rights in your estate plan matters.

Right of PublicityOct 10, 2025

California adds fast injunctive relief and digital replicas to its right of publicity statute

Governor Newsom signed SB 683, amending California Civil Code § 3344 to expressly allow injunctions and temporary restraining orders against unauthorized use of a person's name, voice or likeness — and to include digital replicas in the definitions of voice and likeness.

Why it mattersCalifornians whose identity is misused, including through AI deepfakes, can seek quick court-ordered takedowns — not just money damages later.
Hunton Andrews KurthRead source

Estate PlanningJul 4, 2025

New law sets a permanent $15 million estate and gift tax exemption starting in 2026

Legislation signed July 4, 2025 raised the federal basic exclusion amount to $15 million per person for 2026, with inflation indexing resuming in 2027 and no scheduled sunset. The generation-skipping transfer exemption is also $15 million, and the annual gift exclusion is $19,000 for 2026.

Why it mattersThe feared 2026 drop in the exemption didn't happen, so plans built around it may deserve a review. Wills, trusts, incapacity planning and digital-asset instructions still matter for reasons that have nothing to do with tax.
Internal Revenue ServiceRead source

AI & CopyrightJun 25, 2025

Judge rules Meta's AI training on authors' books was fair use — on that record

Judge Vince Chhabria granted Meta summary judgment, finding its use of authors' books to train Llama “highly transformative” and that the authors had not shown market harm. He stressed the ruling was narrow, warning that AI training that significantly harms the market for original works would be hard to defend as fair use.

Why it mattersTogether with other 2025 rulings, it suggests AI training may be fair use but can lose on evidence of market harm. Creators' records of lost sales and licensing matter.
Perkins CoieRead source

AI & CopyrightJun 11, 2025

Disney and Universal sue Midjourney in the first major studio suit against an AI image generator

Disney and Universal sued Midjourney in federal court in Los Angeles for direct and secondary copyright infringement, alleging the service trains on and outputs their characters, from Darth Vader to the Minions, and ignored their pre-suit letters.

Why it mattersOwners of famous characters are testing whether AI companies are liable for what their tools output — not just what they train on.
NBC NewsRead source

AI & CopyrightMay 9, 2025

U.S. Copyright Office releases Part 3 of its AI report: generative AI training

The Office released a pre-publication version of Part 3, concluding that several stages of AI training implicate copyright owners' rights, that there is no blanket fair use for all training, and that fair use must be decided case by case. It favored letting voluntary licensing markets develop.

Why it mattersIt's the federal copyright agency's most detailed view on whether AI companies need permission to train on creative works, and courts and lawmakers are citing it.
U.S. Copyright OfficeRead source

Trademark & USPTOFeb 26, 2025

Supreme Court limits trademark profit awards to named defendants (Dewberry v. Dewberry)

In a unanimous opinion by Justice Kagan, the Court held that “defendant's profits” under the Lanham Act covers only the named defendant — not unnamed corporate affiliates — throwing out an award of roughly $43 million built on affiliates' profits.

Why it mattersIn a trademark lawsuit, whom you name as defendants can decide how much you can recover.
Supreme Court of the United StatesRead source

AI & CopyrightJan 29, 2025

U.S. Copyright Office releases Part 2 of its AI report: copyrightability

The Office concluded that purely AI-generated material is not protected and that prompts alone generally don't give enough control to make someone an author. Human-authored expression, creative selection and arrangement, and meaningful modifications of AI output can be protected.

Why it mattersThis is the practical rulebook for registering AI-assisted work: protection depends on the human contribution you can identify and disclose.
U.S. Copyright OfficeRead source

Right of PublicitySep 17, 2024

California enacts AB 2602 and AB 1836 to protect performers' digital likenesses

AB 2602 targets contract terms that allow digital replicas of a performer, requiring a reasonably specific description of the intended use and representation by a lawyer or union. AB 1836 bars commercial use of a deceased performer's digital replica without the estate's consent, with exceptions such as news, satire and parody.

Why it mattersCalifornia performers should scrutinize AI and digital-replica clauses in their contracts, and estates of deceased performers gain control over AI recreations.
Office of California Governor Gavin NewsomRead source

Trademark & USPTOJun 13, 2024

Supreme Court upholds ban on registering living people's names without consent (“Trump Too Small”)

In Vidal v. Elster, the Court unanimously upheld the Lanham Act's “names clause,” which bars registering a mark that identifies a particular living person without that person's written consent.

Why it mattersYou generally can't federally register a brand containing a living person's name without their written consent — even as commentary or parody.
Supreme Court of the United StatesRead source

Right of PublicityMar 21, 2024

Tennessee enacts the ELVIS Act, the first state law protecting musicians' voices from AI cloning

Governor Bill Lee signed the ELVIS Act, adding “voice” — both actual and simulated — to Tennessee's personal rights law, which already covered name, photograph and likeness. It took effect July 1, 2024.

Why it mattersIt was the first U.S. law aimed squarely at AI voice clones, and other states and Congress have followed with similar proposals.

What this means for you

Five takeaways from a year of AI law.

  1. Human authorship is the line. Purely AI-generated work can't be registered for copyright in the U.S. Document your creative choices.
  2. Register what you create. Timely registration unlocks statutory damages and attorney's fees — and registered works were at the heart of the largest AI copyright settlement yet.
  3. Your voice and face are property. California, New York and Tennessee have new digital-replica laws, and Congress is weighing a federal right.
  4. Read the AI clauses. Contracts now include digital-replica and AI-training terms. Don't sign them unread — or alone.
  5. Plan for your digital legacy. Name who controls your likeness, accounts and creative rights in your estate plan.
