Essential estate planning documents

The core of every solid plan.

The right documents depend on your state, your property and the size of your estate. For California homeowners, a trust-based estate plan is often recommended to avoid the probate process.

Trusts, explained

Revocable or irrevocable?

A trust is an arrangement where a trustee holds and manages assets for beneficiaries according to your instructions. The right type depends on your goals.

Read: What is a Trust? · Revocable vs. Irrevocable Trust

Revocable living trust

While you’re alive and competent you can be your own trustee and change or cancel the trust. Your successor trustee steps in at incapacity or death and carries out your instructions without needing court approval.

Irrevocable trusts

Generally can’t be changed once signed, but often used for tax planning, asset protection and long-term gifting — and some can be modified through decanting.

Special-purpose trusts

Special needs, pet, charitable, generation-skipping and testamentary trusts — and trusts designed to hold firearms so they pass only to eligible beneficiaries.

Probate

Why most families want to avoid it.

Probate is the court-supervised process of validating a will, gathering assets, paying debts and taxes, and distributing what remains. It’s public, it can be costly, and it can take a minimum of six months to a year or more — even for simple estates.

Read: What is Probate? · Avoid Probate

Probate is only needed for assets left in your individual name. Common ways to avoid it:

  • A properly funded revocable living trust — the most common approach
  • Beneficiary designations on retirement accounts, life insurance and annuities
  • Payable-on-death and transfer-on-death designations
  • Joint ownership with right of survivorship, used carefully

Probate isn’t always the enemy: court oversight can help when family conflict is expected or a will’s validity may be challenged.

What it costs

What does an estate plan cost?

The cost of estate planning varies because every person and situation is different. No two individuals are the same, and no two families have the same goals.

What affects the cost

  • Trust-based or will-based. Trust-based plans generally cost more because they include a living trust, funding guidance and additional documents that help avoid probate. Will-based plans cost less upfront but may lead to higher costs later due to probate.
  • Flat fee or hourly. A flat fee gives you predictable pricing and a clear scope. Hourly billing can climb when an estate is complex or needs multiple revisions.
  • Complexity. Multiple properties, blended families, business ownership and tax planning require more drafting and coordination.
  • Number of properties. Additional properties almost always add cost — especially if they’re outside your home state or outside the country.
  • One person or a couple. A couple’s plan needs coordinated documents for two people, often with mirrored provisions, but not always.

Why flat-fee pricing works well

  • Predictability — no surprise bills
  • Clarity — you know exactly what’s included
  • Efficiency — thoughtful conversations without worrying about hourly charges
  • Transparency — you understand the value of each component

Estate planning FAQ

Honest answers to common questions.

Getting started

What is estate planning?

Estate planning is the process of deciding — in advance and in writing — how you want your assets distributed, who will manage your affairs, who will care for your minor children, and who will make medical and financial decisions on your behalf if you cannot make them yourself. Effective planning protects your family and avoids unnecessary conflict and expense.

Read more: What is an Estate Plan?

Do I need an estate plan?

You are not legally required to have one. But without a plan, you leave decisions about your property to the government — and potentially to family members you don’t choose. If you own property, have children, or care about who receives your assets, you need a plan.

Read more: Preparing for Estate Planning

Isn’t estate planning only for the wealthy?

No. If you own anything — a home, a car, personal belongings, a bank account — you have an estate, and planning ensures those assets go where you want them to go.

Tax is only one part of the picture. California doesn’t impose an estate or inheritance tax for deaths on or after January 1, 2005, and federal estate tax can apply to large estates. Estate planning isn’t about wealth — it’s about protecting the people you love, preserving your legacy, and making sure your wishes are honored.

Read more: Estate Planning Myths

What happens if I die without a will or trust?

That’s called dying “intestate.” Your assets go through probate, and state law decides who inherits — a process called intestate succession. It typically starts with immediate family such as spouses, children, parents and siblings, who are not necessarily the people you would have chosen.

Read more: What is Intestate Succession?

Do I need a lawyer for estate planning?

You never need a lawyer for anything, if you are willing, able, and have the requisite skill to handle a matter yourself. The operative words are “requisite skill.” If you perform a task incorrectly, you pay a lot more than had you invested in a lawyer.

Consulting a competent attorney is advisable for setting up trusts, minimizing taxes, navigating legal intricacies and unique family situations — and it gives you legal advice you can rely on. AI tools can help you get organized, but neither AI nor online forms can account for your unique circumstances.

Read more: How to Choose a Lawyer · AI and Estate Planning

Wills, trusts & key documents

Will or trust — which do I need?

A will outlines how your assets should be distributed and who will care for minor children after your death, but it goes through probate — a public, court-supervised process. A trust is an arrangement in which a trustee manages your assets for your beneficiaries; it can take effect during your lifetime and, when properly funded, often avoids probate and keeps your affairs private.

Not everyone needs a trust-based plan — for some people a will-based plan is sufficient. For California homeowners, a trust-based plan is often recommended to avoid probate. Your consultation will help you decide.

Read more: What is a Trust? · What is a Will · Wills and Trusts: A Quick Reference Guide

Can I change my trust later?

Often, yes. A revocable trust can generally be amended, restated or revoked while you’re alive and not incapacitated, as long as the trust document permits it; after your death it typically becomes irrevocable. An irrevocable trust can’t simply be revoked, but some can be modified through a process called decanting, depending on your state’s law. Because changes can carry tax and legal consequences, talk with an attorney first.

Read more: Updating a Trust — What Does It Mean?

What is a trustee?

A trustee is the person or institution responsible for managing the assets held in a trust, following the instructions in the trust document. The role carries significant legal responsibilities and requires careful judgment. Choosing the right trustee — and a successor trustee — is one of the most important decisions in your estate plan.

Read more: What is a Trustee? · How to Choose a Trustee · Trustee Powers

What is a beneficiary?

A beneficiary is a person or entity designated to receive assets, benefits, or funds from a will, trust, insurance policy, retirement account, or other financial instrument after the owner dies. Beneficiary designations on accounts and policies often override your will, so it’s critical that they’re up to date and consistent with your overall estate plan.

What is a power of attorney?

A power of attorney authorizes someone you trust — your “agent” — to manage your financial affairs if you become incapacitated. A durable power of attorney remains in effect even if you lose mental capacity, making it one of the most critical documents in your plan. Without one, your family may have to go to court for the authority to act for you — a lengthy and expensive process.

Read more: What is a Power of Attorney? · What is an Agent for Healthcare and Finance?

What is a healthcare directive?

An advance healthcare directive (sometimes called a living will) documents your medical treatment wishes if you cannot speak for yourself, and designates a healthcare agent to make decisions on your behalf. Without one, these decisions may fall to family members who disagree — or to medical staff who don’t know your values.

Read more: What is a Healthcare Directive?

What is a HIPAA authorization?

A HIPAA authorization allows your designated representatives to access your protected health information. Without it, even your closest family members may be legally barred from discussing your medical situation with your healthcare providers — even in an emergency.

Read more: What is a HIPAA Authorization?

Incapacity & keeping your plan current

What is incapacity?

Incapacity is a person’s inability to make decisions because of a physical or mental condition. It can result from illness, injury, or cognitive decline, and it may require a designated person — such as your agent under a power of attorney, or a guardian — to make decisions on your behalf. With a plan in place, you, not the courts, choose who is in charge when you need help.

Read more: Incapacity

How often should I review my estate plan?

Generally every 2–4 years. Significant life events may shorten that time frame: marriage or divorce, the birth or adoption of a child, caring for a grandchild, the death or removal of a beneficiary, the death or change of a trustee or executor, moving to a new state, buying or selling real property, changes in your financial situation, or changes in the tax laws.

Read more: Do You Need to Update Your Estate Plan?

Special situations

Can I disinherit a family member?

In California, you generally have the right to disinherit anyone except a spouse, who has community property rights. But it must be done carefully and explicitly. California strongly protects “omitted” heirs: simply leaving a child out — or forgetting to update your plan after a child is born — may still entitle that child to a share.

Name the person and state clearly that the omission is intentional. An unclear or outdated plan can invite legal challenges; an attorney can help you do this in a way that holds up.

Read more: Disinherit a Child: Can You Do It in California?

What is a pet trust?

A pet trust is a legally binding arrangement that provides for the care and financial support of your pet if you become incapacitated or pass away. It designates a caregiver, outlines your pet’s care routine, and sets aside funds to cover their needs. All 50 states now recognize pet trusts. Simply mentioning your pet in your will isn’t enough: a will can’t require anyone to actually take your pet.

Read more: What is a Pet Trust?

What happens to my digital assets?

Digital assets — cryptocurrency, online accounts, digital photos, and intellectual property — require specific planning. Without clear instructions and access credentials left in a secure, legally compliant way, they can be permanently lost; a lost cryptocurrency key can’t be reset. Your estate plan should address digital assets explicitly and authorize your fiduciary to access and manage them.

Read more: Cryptocurrency and Your Estate Plan

How are firearms handled in my estate plan?

Firearms need special attention, because transferring them incorrectly — even to a family member — can violate federal and California law. Not all beneficiaries are legally permitted to own firearms, and weapons regulated under the National Firearms Act (NFA), such as suppressors or registered machine guns, have additional transfer requirements. A properly drafted trust can hold firearms and help ensure they pass to eligible beneficiaries without triggering an illegal transfer.

What is a trust mill, and how do I avoid one?

A trust mill is a sales operation — often run by non-attorneys — that sells cookie-cutter estate planning documents at seminars, hotel luncheons or “free dinner” events, frequently while upselling high-commission annuities or insurance. The documents are often incomplete, poorly drafted, or never properly funded, so your family may still face probate even though you “have a trust.” In California, non-attorneys cannot draft legal documents or give legal advice.

Avoid high-pressure sales, never provide sensitive information at a seminar, verify credentials with the State Bar of California, and work with a licensed attorney who takes time to understand your individual needs.

Read more: Trust Mill Scams

These answers are general information, largely about California law, and are not legal advice. Every family’s situation is different — bring your questions to a consultation.
