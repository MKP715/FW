What's in a complete plan

Six documents that protect everything.

Revocable living trust

The heart of most California plans. Holds your assets during life and passes them privately, often without probate.

Pour-over will

A safety net that catches any assets not formally transferred into your trust — and names guardians for minor children.

Durable power of attorney

Lets someone you trust handle finances if you can't. Without one, family may need a court guardianship.

Advance healthcare directive

Names your healthcare agent and records your wishes for medical care.

HIPAA authorization

Allows the people you choose to talk with your doctors and access your records.

Deeds, assignments & certificate of trust

The paperwork that actually funds your trust — real estate, accounts and personal property.

Trusts, explained

Revocable or irrevocable?

A trust is an arrangement where a trustee holds assets for beneficiaries according to your instructions. The right type depends on your goals.

Revocable living trust

You stay in control as trustee, can change it anytime, and your successor trustee steps in at incapacity or death — usually without probate.

Irrevocable trusts

Harder to change, but useful for specific goals such as asset protection, charitable giving and certain tax strategies.

Special-purpose trusts

Special needs, charitable, generation-skipping and testamentary trusts — and trusts designed to hold firearms lawfully under federal and California law.

Probate

Why most families want to avoid it.

Probate is the court-supervised process of gathering assets, paying debts and distributing what remains. It's public, it can be costly, and it can take a minimum of six months to a year or more — even for simple estates.

Probate is only needed for assets left in your individual name. Common ways to avoid it:

  • A properly funded revocable living trust — the most common approach
  • Beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and insurance
  • Payable-on-death and transfer-on-death designations
  • Joint ownership, used carefully

Estate planning FAQ

Honest answers to common questions.

Will or trust — which do I need?

A will doesn't avoid probate; it simply directs the court on how to distribute your assets. A properly funded trust can often avoid probate entirely and keep your affairs private. Not everyone needs a trust-based plan — for some people a will-based plan is sufficient — and your consultation will help you decide.

What happens if I die without a will or trust?

That's dying “intestate.” State law decides who inherits, and your assets go through probate — which may not match what you would have wanted.

Can I change my trust later?

A revocable trust can generally be amended or revoked while you're alive and competent. Irrevocable trusts are harder to change, though some can be modified through techniques like decanting, depending on state law.

How long does probate take?

Probate can take a minimum of six months to a year or more, even for simple estates — and it's a public process.

Does the new $15 million federal exemption mean I don't need a plan?

Most families will never owe federal estate tax, but tax is only one reason to plan. Incapacity, guardianship for children, privacy, probate avoidance and control over your creative and digital assets matter at every income level.

How do I spot a “trust mill”?

Be cautious of high-pressure seminars and one-size-fits-all trust packages sold by non-lawyers. Take your time, talk with family and advisors, and work with a licensed attorney.
