What is a Deed

By Francine D. Ward·July 20, 2026·6 min read

What is a Deed and Why is it Important

A deed is a legally binding document and if you own real property, it is an essential part of an estate plan. A deed transfers ownership of real property from one party (the grantor) to another (the grantee). It is the written instrument that makes a property transfer valid and enforceable.

A proper deed must:

  • Clearly identify the grantor and grantee
  • Include a legal description of the property
  • Contain the grantor’s signature
  • Be delivered to the grantee
  • In most cases, be notarized

While a deed is the document that transfers ownership, recording the deed with the county recorder provides public notice, protects against fraud, and helps establish clear title.

In short: A deed is the document that transfers ownership. Title is the legal right to the property. They work together, but they are not the same.

Types of Deeds

1. Grant Deed

A grant deed transfers ownership and includes two key assurances:

  • The property has not been transferred to anyone else.
  • The property is free from undisclosed encumbrances.

This is the most common deed used in California real estate transactions.

2. Warranty Deed

A warranty deed provides the highest level of protection to the grantee. The grantor guarantees they have clear title and will defend the grantee against any future claims.

Warranty deeds are common in many states, but not typically used in California, where grant deeds are preferred.

3. Quitclaim Deed

A quitclaim deed transfers whatever interest the grantor may have — with no warranties. It does not guarantee clear title.

Quitclaim deeds are often used between family members, divorcing spouses, or to correct title issues.

4. Interspousal Transfer Deed

This deed transfers property between spouses or registered domestic partners. It is commonly used to:

  • Add a spouse to title
  • Remove a spouse from title
  • Transfer property as part of a divorce settlement

These transfers are often exempt from reassessment under California law.

5. Trust Transfer Deed

A trust transfer deed moves property into or out of a revocable living trust. This is essential for proper trust funding, ensuring the property avoids probate and passes according to the trust terms.

6. Transfer on Death (TOD) Deed (California‑specific)

A TOD deed allows a homeowner to name a beneficiary who will receive the property upon the owner’s death — without probate. However, TOD deeds come with strict rules and can create complications if not used carefully

⭐ Why Recording Your Deed Matters

Recording a deed is not what creates ownership — the transfer becomes legally effective once the deed is properly executed and delivered. But recording the deed with the county recorder is what protects that ownership.

Recording matters because it:

1. Creates Public Notice of Ownership

When a deed is recorded, it becomes part of the public record. This tells the world — lenders, buyers, creditors, and anyone researching the property — that you are the legal owner.

Without recordation, someone else could claim an interest in the property without your knowledge.

2. Establishes Priority (First in Time, First in Right)

California follows a race‑notice system. This means the person who records first generally has priority — even if someone else received a deed earlier but failed to record it.

Example: If a fraudulent seller gives two deeds to two different buyers, the buyer who records first may become the legal owner.

Recording protects you from this nightmare scenario.

3. Helps Prevent Fraud and Title Problems

Unrecorded deeds create gaps in the chain of title. Those gaps can lead to:

  • Conflicting ownership claims
  • Delays in refinancing or selling
  • Title insurance complications
  • Litigation

Recording keeps the chain of title clean and verifiable.

4. Ensures Your Estate Plan Works

If you transfer property into a revocable living trust, the deed must be recorded to show the trust is the legal owner. If it isn’t recorded:

  • The property may still be considered in your individual name
  • It may be forced into probate
  • Your trust instructions may not control the transfer

Recording is essential for proper trust funding.

5. Protects Your Heirs and Beneficiaries

Recorded deeds make it easier for your loved ones to:

  • Prove ownership
  • Avoid disputes
  • Sell or refinance the property
  • Administer your trust or estate

Unrecorded deeds often surface only after death — when it’s too late to fix the problem easily

Bona Fide Purchasers.

Who is a bona fide purchaser? It is the person who (1) buys property, (2) for a reasonable amount, and (3) operates under the belief that the property is free and clear of any encumbrances. Encumbrances could be conflicting claims, liens, unidentified mortgages, long-term subleases, etc. Again, looking at the example above, if Buyer A never recorded his deed, Buyer B was not notified of his title. Thus, Buyer B could be declared the lawful property owner if she records hers before Buyer A.

Common Mistakes

1. Not Recording the Deed

Failing to record a deed leaves ownership unclear and exposes the property to:

  • Conflicting claims
  • Fraud
  • Delays in refinancing or selling
  • Probate complications

Recording protects your rights and preserves the chain of title.

2. Using the Wrong Type of Deed

Choosing the wrong deed can unintentionally:

  • Transfer more rights than intended
  • Provide no warranties
  • Trigger tax consequences
  • Create title defects

For example, quitclaim deeds offer no protection, and TOD deeds can create inheritance conflicts if used incorrectly.

3. Incorrect or Incomplete Legal Description

A deed is only valid if the property is accurately described. Common errors include:

  • Missing parcel numbers
  • Incorrect lot boundaries
  • Typos in metes‑and‑bounds descriptions

Even a small mistake can invalidate the transfer or delay future transactions.

4. Not Funding the Trust Properly

One of the most common estate‑planning mistakes: People create a trust but never transfer their property into it.

If the deed transferring the property to the trust is not executed and recorded, the property may:

  • Remain in the individual’s name
  • Be forced into probate
  • Fall outside the trust instructions

A trust only works when it is properly funded.

5. Adding Children to Title

This is one of the most harmful mistakes people make. Adding children to title can trigger:

  • Gift tax issues
  • Loss of step‑up in basis
  • Exposure to the child’s creditors, lawsuits, or divorce
  • Loss of control over the property

A trust is almost always a safer, cleaner solution.

6. Failing to Update Ownership After Life Events

Major life changes require deed updates. Common oversights include:

  • Divorce
  • Marriage
  • Death of a co‑owner
  • Refinancing

Outdated deeds create confusion and can complicate trust administration.

7. DIY Deeds Without Legal Guidance

Online templates and self‑prepared deeds often contain:

  • Incorrect vesting language
  • Missing signatures
  • Wrong deed type
  • Improper notarization

These errors may not surface until refinancing, selling, or settling an estate — when it’s too late to fix easily.

Your home is one of your most valuable assets — make sure it’s protected. Whether you’re transferring property into a trust, updating ownership after a life change, or simply trying to understand what your deed actually says, the details matter. A small mistake can lead to probate, tax consequences, or disputes that your loved ones will have to untangle later.

If you’re unsure whether your deed is correct, recorded, or aligned with your estate plan, now is the time to review it.

When clients ask about deeds, they’re often also navigating other essential planning documents. If you’re sorting out property transfers, it’s equally important to understand how a Power of Attorney works to ensure someone you trust can act on your behalf when needed. And for those with beloved animals, a Pet Trust can provide long‑term protection and peace of mind. These tools work together to create a more complete and secure estate plan.
