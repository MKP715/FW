AI & Copyright

Do you own what AI helps you make?

The Supreme Court has let stand the rule that copyright requires a human author. Here's what that means if you write, paint, design or compose with AI.

By Francine D. Ward·October 17, 2026·7 min read

If you use generative AI in your creative work, you've probably wondered whether you still own the result. After the Supreme Court's decision in March 2026 not to hear Thaler v. Perlmutter, the answer in the United States is clearer than it's ever been: you own the parts you created. The parts a machine created on its own belong to no one.

What the courts decided

Computer scientist Stephen Thaler tried to register a copyright in an image called “A Recent Entrance to Paradise,” listing his AI system as its sole author. The U.S. Copyright Office refused. In March 2025, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit unanimously agreed that the Copyright Act requires a human author — while noting that works made by humans who use AI can still be registered. On March 2, 2026, the Supreme Court declined to review the case, leaving that ruling in place.

The question isn't “Did you use AI?” It's “What did you contribute — and can you show it?”

What the Copyright Office says

In January 2025, the Copyright Office published Part 2 of its report on copyright and artificial intelligence, focused on copyrightability. Its conclusions, in plain English:

  • Purely AI-generated material isn't protected.
  • Prompts alone generally aren't enough. Typing instructions usually doesn't give you enough control over the output to make you its author.
  • Human contributions are protected. Your own writing, drawing or composing; your creative selection and arrangement of material; and meaningful modifications you make to AI output can all qualify.
  • Using AI as a tool doesn't disqualify you. Just as a camera doesn't stop a photograph from being yours, an AI-assisted workflow doesn't by itself defeat copyright.

When you register a work that contains more than a trivial amount of AI-generated material, the Office expects you to disclose it and to describe the human-authored contribution you're claiming. The Office's 2023 decision on the comic book Zarya of the Dawn is a good illustration: the author's text and her selection and arrangement of the images were protected, but the individual AI-generated images were not.

A practical checklist for creators

  1. Keep your process. Save drafts, sketches, edit histories and notes that show your creative decisions.
  2. Disclose and claim correctly. On registration applications, exclude AI-generated material and describe what you contributed. An inaccurate application can put your registration at risk.
  3. Read your AI tool's terms. Who owns outputs? Can the company train on what you upload? Are you indemnified if an output infringes someone else's work?
  4. Check your contracts. Many publishing and client agreements include warranties that your work is original. Some now address AI use directly. Make sure what you promise matches how you work.
  5. Protect confidential work. Think twice before pasting unpublished manuscripts, client materials or legal questions into public AI tools. In 2026 a federal judge ruled that one defendant's conversations with a consumer AI chatbot were not protected by attorney-client privilege.
  6. Register what matters. Timely registration preserves statutory damages and attorney's fees — leverage you'll want if your work is copied.

The other side: when AI is trained on your work

Ownership of outputs is only half the story. Courts are also deciding whether AI companies need permission to train on copyrighted works. The early results are mixed: in 2025, two federal judges in California found AI training on books to be fair use on the records before them, while emphasizing that market harm could change the outcome; a $1.5 billion class settlement followed claims involving pirated books; and in 2026 a federal appeals court held that copying legal headnotes to train a competing AI tool was not fair use. Follow these cases in the IP & AI Law Watch.

Bottom line: AI can be a powerful creative partner, but copyright protects people. Document your contribution, register strategically, and get advice before you sign contracts or file applications involving AI-assisted work.
