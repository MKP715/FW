Right of Publicity

Your voice, your likeness, your legacy

AI can now clone a voice from seconds of audio. New laws are catching up — and your estate plan should, too.

From the Law Office of Francine D. Ward·October 17, 2026·8 min read

Your name, face and voice aren’t just who you are — under the law, they’re property. In the age of AI, that property has never been more valuable, or easier to take. Here’s how the law protects your identity while you’re living, what happens after you’re gone, and the steps worth taking now.

Your identity is property

There is no single federal right of publicity in the United States. Protection comes from state law, and California’s is among the strongest. California Civil Code section 3344 protects living people against unauthorized commercial use of their name, voice, signature, photograph or likeness. Section 3344.1 protects “deceased personalities” for 70 years after death — which means these rights can be owned, licensed, transferred and inherited.

In 2025, California went further. SB 683 amended section 3344 to expressly allow injunctions and temporary restraining orders against unauthorized uses, and it brought digital replicas within the definitions of voice and likeness.

How AI changed the stakes

  • Tennessee’s ELVIS Act (2024) was the first law aimed squarely at AI voice clones, adding “voice” — actual or simulated — to the state’s personal rights law.
  • California AB 2602 and AB 1836 (2024) require that contract terms allowing a digital replica of a performer describe the intended use with reasonable specificity, and bar commercial use of a deceased performer’s digital replica without the estate’s consent, subject to exceptions.
  • New York (2025) requires disclosure of AI “synthetic performers” in ads and consent from heirs or executors before a deceased person’s likeness is used commercially.
  • The federal TAKE IT DOWN Act now requires covered platforms to remove nonconsensual intimate images — including AI-generated ones — within 48 hours of a valid request.
  • The NO FAKES Act, which would create a federal right to control digital replicas, cleared the Senate Judiciary Committee in June 2026 but has not become law.
If you don’t decide who controls your likeness, the law — or a stranger with an AI tool — will decide for you.

Estates are already on the front lines

In October 2025, OpenAI paused AI videos of Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. on its Sora app at the request of his estate. In 2026, filmmakers released a trailer featuring an AI recreation of the late Val Kilmer — with, they said, his estate’s approval and compensation. Families are now making decisions their loved ones never imagined. The best gift you can give them is clear instructions.

While you’re living

  1. Read every contract for AI and digital-replica language. Look for words like “simulation,” “synthetic,” “digital double,” “voice model” or rights “in all media now known or hereafter devised.”
  2. Protect your name as a brand. If you do business under your name, consider trademark registration.
  3. Act fast on misuse. Document it, use platform takedown tools, and talk to a lawyer about demand letters or court orders.

In your estate plan

  1. Assign your intellectual property and publicity rights to your trust so they pass smoothly and privately. Trusts can hold copyrights, trademarks, trade secrets, patents and publicity rights.
  2. Name a decision-maker — someone who understands your work and values — to approve or refuse uses of your name, voice and likeness.
  3. Leave written instructions. Do you want AI recreations never, only for certain purposes, or only with approval from specific people? Say so.
  4. Inventory your digital life. Accounts, archives, recordings, domains and the credentials your fiduciaries will need.
  5. Review where you live. Post-mortem rights vary from state to state and can depend on where you were domiciled at death.
