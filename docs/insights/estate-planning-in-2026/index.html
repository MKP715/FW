Estate planning in 2026: what changed — and what didn’t

The federal exemption is now $15 million and permanent. Here’s why you still need a plan, and when to update the one you have.

From the Law Office of Francine D. Ward·October 17, 2026·5 min read

For years, families braced for the federal estate tax exemption to be cut roughly in half at the start of 2026. It didn’t happen. Here’s what changed, and why it doesn’t mean you can skip planning.

What changed

  • The basic exclusion is $15 million per person for 2026 under legislation signed July 4, 2025, with inflation adjustments resuming in 2027 and no scheduled sunset. Married couples can effectively shelter about $30 million.
  • The generation-skipping transfer exemption is also $15 million.
  • The annual gift exclusion is $19,000 per recipient for 2026.

What didn’t change

Most families were never going to owe federal estate tax. They plan for other reasons — and those reasons are exactly the same in 2026:

  • Incapacity. Without a durable power of attorney and healthcare directive, your family may need a court guardianship to help you.
  • Probate. In California, probate can take a minimum of six months to a year or more and becomes a public record. A funded trust can usually avoid it.
  • Children. A will names guardians for minor children; a trust controls when and how they inherit.
  • Creative and digital assets. Copyrights, royalties, trademarks, your likeness, online accounts and cryptocurrency all need instructions.
Tax is only one reason to plan. Love is the bigger one.

When to review your plan

Revisit your documents after a marriage, divorce, birth, adoption or death in the family; a move to another state; a new business, book deal or significant IP; or, generally, every two to four years. Plans built around the old, lower exemption — especially those with formula clauses — deserve a fresh look.

A word of caution

Be wary of “trust mills”: high-pressure seminars and one-size-fits-all packages sold by non-lawyers. Take your time, talk with your family and advisors, and work with a licensed attorney.
